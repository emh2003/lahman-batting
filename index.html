How the Game Changed

By Emily · Data: Lahman Baseball Database, 1871–2025

This report uses the batting table of the Lahman Baseball Database: 128,597 player-seasons from 24,011 players, covering every season from 1871 through 2025. Following the AL, the NL, and the 19th-century major leagues decade by decade, it finds a game that traded contact for power. Home runs per plate appearance are more than eight times as common as in the 1900s, strikeouts have doubled since the 1950s, and batting averages have fallen to their lowest level since 1968, so that one in three plate appearances now ends without the ball in play. Over the same stretch the league nearly tripled in size, became far more international, and, after the 2023 rule changes, started running again. A final section looks at the Negro Leagues, whose hitters matched the majors before integration.

1st inning

Home runs are more than eight times as common as in the dead-ball era

In the 1900s, hitters averaged just 2.2 home runs per 600 plate appearances (roughly one full season for an everyday player). Babe Ruth's era tripled that to 6.2 in the 1920s, and the number has climbed in fits and starts ever since, reaching 18.6 in the 2020s, 8.4 times the 1900s rate. The single-season peak came in 2019, at 21.8 home runs per 600 plate appearances.

Because this is a rate per plate appearance, the rise isn't just the result of more teams or longer seasons. Each trip to the plate is far more likely to end in a home run than it used to be.

2nd inning

Strikeouts have doubled since the 1950s, while walks stayed flat

In the 1950s, 11.4% of plate appearances ended in a strikeout. In the 2020s, 22.7% do, twice the rate. The highest season on record is 2020, at 23.4%, though that was a shortened 60-game season. The climb was steady rather than sudden: strikeout rates rose in almost every decade after World War II.

Walks tell a different story. The walk rate was 9.3% in the 1950s and 8.5% in the 2020s, so nearly all of the change in how plate appearances end comes from strikeouts, not walks.

3rd inning

Batting averages have fallen to their lowest level since 1968

The league batting average peaked at .271 in 1999, at the height of a high-offense era. It has fallen since, to .243 in both 2022 and 2024, the lowest of any season since 1968. That year, known as "the Year of the Pitcher," is also the low point since 1901, at .237. It was .245 in 2025.

The long-run swings are large. The best season since 1901 was 1930, when the whole league hit .296. Today's hitters trade average for power: fewer hits, but more of them leave the park.

4th inning

One in three plate appearances now ends without the ball in play

A home run, a walk, and a strikeout have one thing in common: the fielders never touch the ball. Analysts call these the "three true outcomes." In the 1950s they made up 22.9% of plate appearances. In the 2020s they make up 34.3%, or about one in every three.

This finding ties the first two together. More home runs and more strikeouts, with walks roughly unchanged, add up to a game with noticeably less action for the defense.

5th inning

Singles have gone from four in five hits to under two in three

When hitters do get a hit, it's more likely to go for extra bases than it used to be. In the 1900s, 79.3% of all hits were singles. In the 2020s, 64.4% are. The rest are doubles, triples, and, more and more, home runs.

This is the other side of the falling batting average. Hitters now swing for damage and accept more outs in exchange for more extra-base hits.

6th inning

Stolen bases jumped 41% the year the rules changed

Base stealing had been fading. Leaving out the shortened 2020 season, the total fell to 2,213 steals in 2021, the fewest since the league reached 30 teams in 1998. In 2023, MLB introduced larger bases, a pitch clock, and limits on pickoff throws. Steals jumped from 2,486 in 2022 to 3,503 in 2023, a 41% increase in a single season.

The increase has held: steals rose again to 3,617 in 2024, the most of any season shown, and there were 3,440 in 2025. This is the clearest case in the data of a rule change reshaping how the game is played almost immediately.

7th inning

A third of plate appearances now go to players born outside the U.S.

In the 1870s, about one in ten plate appearances (10.1%) went to players born abroad, mostly in Canada, Ireland, and England. That share bottomed out at 1.2% in the 1920s and 1.1% in the 1930s. By the 1960s that was 13.1%, and in the 2020s it's 35.1%. The Dominican Republic (11.0% of all 2020s plate appearances) and Venezuela (9.5%) lead, followed by Cuba (3.8%) and Puerto Rico (3.0%).

The biggest jumps came in the 1990s (+6.2 points) and 2000s (+9.3 points), when teams were expanding their scouting across Latin America.

8th inning

The league nearly tripled in size

In 1925, 526 different players came to bat for 16 teams. In 2025, 1,470 players did, for 30 teams. Expansion added teams in waves from 1961 to 1998, and each wave shows up as a step in the chart.

The number of players has grown faster than the number of teams. More players now get at least one at-bat each season, which fits with clubs moving players between the majors and the minors more often.

9th inning

Before integration, Negro Leagues hitters matched the majors

In 2020, MLB recognized seven Negro Leagues from 1920 to 1948 as major leagues, and the Lahman data now includes them. Over those seasons, Negro Leagues hitters batted .274, compared with .275 in the AL and NL. Their on-base percentage was .338 (vs. .340) and their OPS was .717 (vs. .728).

The records are much less complete: they cover 620,798 plate appearances, compared with 2,776,226 in the AL/NL over the same years, and teams played shorter schedules. Even so, the numbers show players who were shut out of the majors hitting at essentially the same level, until Jackie Robinson's debut in 1947 began to change the game for good.

About the data

Where it comes from

The data is the batting table of the Lahman Baseball Database, created by Sean Lahman and now maintained by the Society for American Baseball Research (SABR). This release covers 1871–2025 and is licensed under CC BY-SA 3.0. Player names, batting hand, and birth country come from the database's People table. Team names and franchise codes come from its Teams table.

What one row is

One row is one player's batting line for one team in one season. A player traded mid-season has one row per team. The clean file, data/batting.csv, has 128,597 rows and 34 columns.

Rows dropped

One of the 128,598 raw rows was dropped: a 1912 record (player tayloci99, team WBS) that lists 0 hits but 1 home run, which can't both be true. Unless a section says otherwise, the findings use only the 117,141 rows in the AL, the NL, and the 19th-century major leagues. The 9th inning also uses the Negro Leagues rows from 1920–1948.

How the rates are computed

Every rate is computed from summed totals across all the rows in a season or decade, never by averaging each player's own rate. Blank cells mean a stat wasn't recorded and are not treated as zero, except that a blank walk, hit-by-pitch, sacrifice hit, or sacrifice fly counts as 0 when adding up plate appearances, and a blank hit-by-pitch or sacrifice fly counts as 0 in on-base percentage.

  • Plate appearances (PA) = AB + BB + HBP + SH + SF
  • Batting average = H ÷ AB
  • On-base percentage = (H + BB + HBP) ÷ (AB + BB + HBP + SF)
  • Slugging = total bases ÷ AB; OPS = OBP + slugging
  • HR per 600 PA = HR ÷ PA × 600
  • Strikeout rate = SO ÷ PA, using only rows where strikeouts were recorded; walk rate = BB ÷ PA
  • Three true outcomes = (HR + BB + SO) ÷ PA, using only rows where strikeouts were recorded
  • Singles share = singles ÷ hits, where singles = H − 2B − 3B − HR
  • Born abroad = birth country is not USA (players with an unknown birth country count as not born abroad)

Reproducing the numbers

scripts/build_data.py builds the clean file from the raw tables. scripts/report_numbers.py computes every number in this report and writes the chart data to data/report.json. You can also check the rate numbers on the dashboard: set the league group to "AL/NL and 19th-century majors," pick a measure, and set the time grain to decade or season.
